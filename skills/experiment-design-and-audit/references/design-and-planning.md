# Design and Plan an Experiment

## Make the question discriminating

Inspect prior evidence, existing protocols, implementations, materials, and
constraints. Define the decision to be informed and what observation would change
it. Use the shared claim record to make the research question operational before
selecting a design or metric. Do not silently turn a narrow comparison into a
mechanism, universal superiority, or causal claim.

Map each central claim to its experiment, comparator, required controls,
observations, analysis, and acceptance evidence. Separate establishing an effect,
explaining a mechanism, and testing generalization. Prefer experiments that
distinguish plausible alternatives; avoid expensive activity with no decision
it can resolve.

## Select a design with an identifiable contrast

- Specify factors, levels/ranges, intentional differences, nuisance variables,
  and impossible/constrained combinations. Justify the tested operating range
  and unit/task/workload selection against the intended scope.
- Choose assignment, randomization, blocking, pairing, counterbalancing, blinding,
  or justified alternatives based on the actual threats. Specify how allocation
  and order are produced, concealed, and recorded where applicable.
- Distinguish independent replication from repeated measurement of the same unit.
  Address clustering, temporal dependence, learning/fatigue, carryover, shared
  environments, and interference when these can affect the contrast.
- When factors/components change together, consider interactions and attribution.
  A whole-system comparison does not isolate one component. Reduced/fractional
  designs must identify important unresolved or aliased effects; one-factor-at-
  a-time comparisons cannot by themselves identify interactions.
- Without randomized assignment, identify selection and time effects, the causal
  identification argument if any, adjustment assumptions, and residual confounding.
  Narrow causal language when the design cannot defend it. Correlational evidence
  can still answer a descriptive or predictive question.
- Select positive/negative controls, reference standards/implementations, and
  manipulation checks where they expose a relevant failure. Specify expected
  behavior, tolerance, and the consequence of control failure. Do not require a
  control that cannot distinguish a live alternative.

## Specify measurement and analysis

Define instrument/harness, calibration or validation checks, measurement resolution,
uncertainty, timing boundaries, sampling/window, valid ranges, detection limits,
and observer/instrumentation effects as applicable. Retain component measurements
when a derived ratio or composite could conceal an error or tradeoff. Explain why
the measure reflects the construct: speed alone is not productivity if output
quality differs.

Before target outcomes are inspected, specify:

- Primary/secondary outcomes, estimands or descriptive summaries, aggregation and
  weighting, effect sizes, practical decision thresholds/margins, and uncertainty.
- Inclusion/exclusion, missingness, timeouts, censoring, non-completion,
  nonadherence, outliers, retries, replacement, and the resulting target population.
  Preserve the difference between assigned treatment and successful completers.
- The estimator/model or testing framework and assumptions, including pairing,
  clusters, repeated measures, and distributions. Name conditional fallback
  analyses and their triggers instead of selecting whichever yields a desired
  outcome. No universal statistical test or confidence level fits every question.
- Multiplicity across outcomes/conditions/subgroups, repeated interim looks,
  adaptive selection, and stopping rules where relevant. A Bayesian approach also
  needs an explicit model, prior choices, and a defensible decision procedure.
- Sample/replicate allocation justified by meaningful effect or desired precision,
  plausible variability and dependence, attrition, and cost. Pilot estimates are
  estimates. Do not invent a universal number of runs, subjects, or seeds. If
  resources cannot resolve the question, state the limit or redesign it.

Separate exploration from confirmation without discouraging exploration. Record
which outcomes were already inspected when a choice was made. Preserve dated
amendments, their reasons, and analysis consequences. An external preregistration
can help where appropriate but is not mandatory for every task. A follow-up with
fresh or appropriately protected evidence may confirm an exploratory finding;
rewriting the old protocol does not.

## Make the plan executable and reviewable

Provide the condition matrix, units/tasks/workloads, allocation/order, independent
replications, control checks, preparation, collection procedure, analysis procedure,
and required artifacts. Assign roles where people must coordinate. Record relevant
approvals, consent, specialist input, or operational prerequisites when the study
actually needs them; do not fabricate domain authority.

Plan a bounded pilot when feasibility, measurement sensitivity, manipulation, or
variance assumptions need checking. State what it can establish and how pilot
evidence will be used without contaminating the target evaluation. Reserve
resources for justified diagnostics and validation. Specify collection limits,
stop conditions, interruption/recovery, and reproducibility needs.

Give a scoped readiness decision. Surface every unresolved choice that can
change a central interpretation, its consequence, and the evidence/decision
needed. Allow independent preparation to continue. Reserve extension paths by
recording follow-up questions, needed evidence, and reconsideration triggers;
do not defer the current comparison's essential controls to a later study.

[blocking](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/pri/section3/pri332.htm),

Use them as background; this procedure does not certify a particular study.
