# Academic Paper Writing

Match the author's intended contribution, audience, language, and venue. Use the structure appropriate to the discipline and the requested deliverable.

## Build the argument from evidence

1. Identify the research question, the gap that motivates it, the contribution, and the evidence available. Keep each main claim within the scope of that evidence.
2. Outline the paper in the order readers need the information. For each section, identify the question it answers, the concepts it needs, and the evidence or reasoning it contributes. Use methods and results to support empirical claims, assumptions and proofs to support theoretical claims, and selection criteria and sources to support a synthesis.
3. Draft from the supplied material and verified sources. Preserve the author's technical meaning when revising. Treat measured results, interpretations, and proposed explanations distinctly. Flag missing evidence, citations, or author decisions in the draft and keep related claims provisional. Never invent findings, methods, or bibliographic details.

## Define every concept before use

Readers must know what each concept means before interpreting any statement that depends on it. Use familiar language to establish the intended meaning of every technical or paper-specific concept in the text. Include named constructs, methods, entities, acronyms, variables, notation, metrics, and categories. Expanding an acronym or citing a source still leaves the paper responsible for explaining the meaning it uses.

- **Order by dependency.** Define the concepts a definition relies on first. The defining sentence can introduce and name the concept. Follow it with claims, comparisons, equations, or instructions that depend on it. Unpack circular definitions into terms the reader already understands.
- **Place the definition where it becomes necessary.** Give central concepts an accessible definition in the introduction before using them to frame the question or contribution. Define a specialized concept only where readers first need it, such as a sampling rule or metric in Methods, or a proof-specific symbol before the statement that uses it. When a high-level meaning is needed early, give that meaning locally and put the formal or operational detail before its first technical use. Keep the meanings consistent across levels of detail.
- **Make the definition precise enough for the use.** Identify what the term denotes and state any boundaries that affect interpretation. For a metric, give the population or denominator, units, and aggregation when relevant. For notation, state what each symbol denotes and its domain where needed. Use the same term or symbol for the same meaning within its scope. Introduce a distinct name or symbol when the meaning changes.
- **Account for independent reading.** Give the abstract its own short definitions, and establish terms again when first needed in the main text. Use accessible wording in the title and headings if a term would otherwise need explanation there. Give figures, tables, and independently read supplements enough local context to interpret their terms, labels, and symbols. Close the gap locally before referring readers to a longer definition elsewhere.

For example, in Methods, explain that a request counts as a timeout when it receives no response within the configured time limit. Then define the timeout rate as the number of timed-out requests divided by the total number of requests sent. Put both definitions before the first comparison that uses that rate. If the abstract needs only the main finding, express it in plain language such as “a smaller share of requests received no response within the time limit.”

## Check the draft in reading order

- Trace each concept from its first use to its definition across prose, headings, equations, captions, tables, and other material in scope. For a long draft, track the term, definition location, first dependent use, and any separately read material that needs a local definition.
- Repair gaps by defining the concept at the appropriate point, moving the dependent passage, or expressing the early point in terms already established. Recheck affected passages after moving or renaming material. If working on an excerpt, use earlier definitions only when the manuscript context establishes them. Flag any unverified dependency and give the author the proposed placement when the necessary edit falls outside the requested scope.
- Verify that each major claim follows from the evidence or reasoning presented, that results and terminology agree across sections, and that limitations qualify the conclusions they affect. Deliver the manuscript or requested passage with any unresolved issues identified separately.
